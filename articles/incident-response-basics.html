Incident Response for Beginners: What To Do When Something Goes Wrong

Learn the calm, step-by-step way organisations deal with a security incident.

What is an incident?

A security incident is an event that harms, or could harm, the confidentiality, integrity or availability of an organisation's information or systems. A stolen laptop, a staff member who entered their password on a fake page, ransomware that locks files, and a website taken offline are all incidents. Incident response (IR) is the organised way of dealing with them.

The aim is not only to stop the attack. It is to limit the damage, restore normal work, and learn enough that it is less likely to happen again.

The phases of incident response

A widely taught model comes from the US standards body NIST. Its older guide describes four phases, and many teams still use them:

  1. Preparation: have people, tools, contact lists and written plans ready before anything happens.
  2. Detection and analysis: notice a problem, decide whether it is real, and work out what it affects.
  3. Containment, eradication and recovery: stop it spreading, remove the cause, and bring systems back safely.
  4. Post-incident activity: review what happened and improve.

NIST published a newer revision of this guide in 2025 that fits incident response into its wider Cybersecurity Framework, which the What Is Cybersecurity? article mentions. The idea stays the same: prepare, detect, respond, recover and learn.

Containment: stop the spread first

Containment means limiting how far the problem can reach. Typical steps are isolating an infected laptop from the network, disabling a compromised account, blocking a malicious address, or taking a service offline. Choose the least disruptive step that works, but do not hesitate when the risk is high.

Do not wipe or switch off a machine in a hurry. Evidence such as running programs and logs can disappear. Isolate first, record what you see, and follow your team's evidence rules, because the material may be needed to understand the attack or for legal reasons.

Eradication and recovery

  • Eradication: remove the cause, such as malware, a bad account or the weakness that let the attacker in.
  • Recovery: restore from clean backups, change passwords and keys that may be exposed, and watch closely for the attacker's return.

Severity and who to tell

Teams rate incidents, for example low, medium, high and critical, based on how much data, how many people and how important the systems are. Higher severity means faster action and more people informed. Know who must be told: your manager, the security lead, the legal team, and sometimes customers or regulators. Rules about reporting a data breach differ by country, so organisations check with their legal advisers.

A simple playbook

A playbook is a checklist for one kind of incident, so people do not have to invent a plan under pressure. Here is one for a reported phishing email: 

PLAYBOOK: Reported phishing email

1. Collect    Get the original email (as an attachment), sender, time, who received it
2. Check      Is there a link or attachment? Has anyone clicked or opened it?
3. Contain    Remove the email from all mailboxes; block the sender and any bad link
4. Assess     If someone clicked or entered a password, treat that account as exposed
5. Recover    Reset the password, sign the user out everywhere, check for new mail rules
6. Notify     Tell the affected users and the security lead
7. Learn      Record what happened and what would have stopped it sooner

Keep a timeline

Write down what happened and when, as it happens. A good timeline looks like this: 

09:02  User reports a suspicious email to the security mailbox
09:10  Analyst confirms 14 staff received it; 2 clicked the link
09:18  Email removed from all mailboxes; link blocked
09:25  Passwords reset for the 2 users; sessions signed out
09:40  Mailbox rules checked: no forwarding rules found
10:15  Staff notified; incident ticket updated with timeline

Use one time zone for everything, and record facts rather than guesses. If you are unsure, write "unconfirmed".

Checking files safely

Sometimes you need to know whether a file is the same as a known one. A hash is a fingerprint of a file's contents, so identical files give identical hashes. Our Hash Generator shows how this works with text. Never open a suspicious file on your own computer. Follow your organisation's process and use an isolated environment.

After the incident

Hold a lessons-learned meeting soon after. Ask what happened, what worked, what slowed you down and what to change. The aim is to improve the system, not to blame a person. A blame-free review makes people report problems quickly, which is what you want.

Common beginner mistakes

  • Panicking and changing many things at once, so nobody knows what helped.
  • Not writing notes during the incident.
  • Wiping a machine before saving evidence.
  • Restoring systems before removing the cause, so the attacker returns.
  • Skipping the lessons-learned review.
  • Blaming the person who clicked, so others stop reporting.

Practice project

Write a playbook for a lost or stolen laptop, in the same numbered style. Include who to tell, how to lock or wipe it remotely, which passwords to change and what to record. Then invent a small incident and write a five-line timeline for it.

Summary

  • Incident response is the organised handling of security incidents.
  • The phases are prepare, detect and analyse, contain, eradicate and recover, then learn.
  • Contain first, but preserve evidence.
  • Playbooks and timelines keep people calm and consistent.
  • Review without blame, and improve.
