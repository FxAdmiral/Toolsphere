How Does Cloud Computing Work? A Beginner's Guide

Understand what "the cloud" really is, in plain English.

Published 27 October 2026 • About 7 minutes to read

The short answer: cloud computing means renting computing power, storage and software over the internet from a provider's data centres, instead of buying and running your own machines. You use what you need and pay for what you use.

What is cloud computing?

Years ago, a business that wanted a website or an app bought its own servers, put them in a room and hired people to look after them. Today it can rent that power from a company that already owns thousands of servers. That rented, internet-delivered computing is the cloud. The "cloud" is not a mysterious place in the sky. It is real computers in real buildings, owned by someone else.

A good comparison is electricity. You do not build a power station to run your lights. You plug in and pay for the electricity you use. The cloud offers computing the same way.

How does it work?

  1. Data centres: providers run huge buildings full of servers, storage and networking equipment, in many parts of the world.
  2. Virtualisation: software splits one powerful physical machine into many separate virtual machines, so many customers can share the hardware safely and each sees their own private computer.
  3. Self-service: you ask for what you need through a website or a program, and it is ready in minutes, without phoning anyone.
  4. Pay for use: usage is measured, and you are billed for what you used, such as hours of computing or gigabytes stored.
  5. Scaling: when more people visit, you can add capacity, and remove it again when they leave.

These ideas match the widely quoted definition from the US standards body NIST: on-demand self-service, access over the network, pooled resources, rapid elasticity and measured service.

The three main service models

  • IaaS (Infrastructure as a Service): you rent the basic building blocks, such as virtual machines, storage and networks, and manage the software on them yourself. It gives the most control and needs the most skill.
  • PaaS (Platform as a Service): the provider also manages the operating system and runtime. You just deploy your code, for example a Node.js app.
  • SaaS (Software as a Service): you simply use finished software through a browser or app, such as email, online documents or video meetings. You manage nothing but your own account and data.

You probably use SaaS every day without noticing. When you open Gmail or watch a video online, you are using the cloud.

Types of cloud

  • Public cloud: shared infrastructure run by a provider and rented by many customers. The best known providers are Amazon Web Services (AWS), Microsoft Azure and Google Cloud.
  • Private cloud: infrastructure used by only one organisation, either in its own building or hosted for it.
  • Hybrid cloud: a mix of the two, often used when some data must stay on the organisation's own systems.

Regions and availability zones

Providers divide their data centres into regions, which are geographic areas such as "Europe West" or "US East", and each region has several separate availability zones. Choosing a region close to your users makes your service faster. Spreading across zones keeps it running if one data centre fails. The region you choose can also matter for laws about where personal data may be stored.

Why people use the cloud

  • Lower start-up cost. No need to buy hardware first.
  • Speed. A server can be ready in minutes.
  • Scalability. Handle a busy day without owning machines for it all year.
  • Reliability. Backups and multiple data centres are easier to arrange.
  • Access from anywhere with an internet connection.

The downsides

  • Costs can surprise you. Pay-as-you-go can grow fast if something is left running or misconfigured. Set budgets and billing alerts.
  • You depend on the internet and the provider. Outages happen, even at the biggest providers.
  • Lock-in. Moving from one provider to another can be hard work.
  • Security mistakes are easy to make (see below).

Cloud security: the shared responsibility model

Security in the cloud is shared. The provider protects the physical buildings, hardware and the basic platform. You are responsible for how you use it: your passwords, who has access, your settings and your data. How the split falls depends on the service model: with SaaS the provider does most of the work, and with IaaS you do much more.

Most real cloud breaches come from customer mistakes, not from the provider's data centres being broken into. The common ones are:

  • Storage left open to the whole internet by accident.
  • Weak passwords and no multi-factor authentication. Use our Password Generator and turn on multi-factor sign-in.
  • Secret keys written into code and pushed to a public repository. Read the Git and GitHub guide to see how to avoid it.
  • Giving accounts far more permission than they need. Give each person and program the least access that works.
  • No logs or alerts, so nobody notices a problem. See Logs, Alerts and SIEM.

Where have you already met the cloud on ToolSphere?

The tools on ToolSphere run in your own browser, so your data does not go to a server. But when the site is published for everyone, it will be stored on a hosting service, which is the cloud at work. Common beginner mistakes

  • Thinking "the cloud" is automatically safe or automatically private.
  • Forgetting to switch off test machines and getting a bill.
  • Using the account's all-powerful root login for everyday work.
  • Not reading the free-tier limits before starting. Check each provider's current terms.

How to start learning

  1. Use SaaS tools thoughtfully and notice what you store where.
  2. Create a free account with one provider and read its free-tier rules first.
  3. Set a budget alert before you create anything.
  4. Host a simple static website, then try a small Node.js app.
  5. Delete what you no longer need.

Summary

  • Cloud computing is renting computing power, storage and software over the internet.
  • Virtualisation lets many customers safely share the same hardware.
  • IaaS, PaaS and SaaS differ in how much you manage yourself.
  • Security is shared: the provider secures the platform, you secure your use of it.
  • Watch your costs, and protect access and data.
