What Does a SOC Analyst Do? A Beginner's Guide

Meet the people who watch for attacks around the clock, and learn how to think like one.

What is a SOC?

A Security Operations Center (SOC) is a team, and often a room full of screens, whose job is to watch an organisation's systems for signs of attack, and to respond quickly when something is wrong. Banks, hospitals, universities, telecom companies and governments all run one, and many smaller companies pay an outside provider to run theirs.

Think of it as the security guard room of a building. The cameras are logs and sensors, the guards are analysts, and the procedure for "what do we do if the alarm goes off" is the incident response plan.

What does a SOC analyst do?

  • Monitor alerts and dashboards from security tools.
  • Triage each alert: decide whether it is a real threat, harmless, or unclear.
  • Investigate suspicious activity by searching logs and checking related systems.
  • Escalate real incidents to more senior people, with clear notes.
  • Document everything, so the next person understands what happened.
  • Improve detections by reporting noisy or missed alerts.

Most of the job is careful checking and clear writing, not dramatic hacker-versus-hacker scenes. A good analyst is curious, calm and methodical.

Common levels in a SOC

Titles differ from company to company, but many SOCs use tiers:

  • Tier 1 (triage): reviews incoming alerts, closes false alarms and escalates real ones. This is where most beginners start.
  • Tier 2 (investigation): digs deeper into escalated incidents and helps contain them.
  • Tier 3 (advanced): hunts for threats that tools missed, studies attackers' methods and improves detection.

Key terms to learn first

  • Event: anything that happens on a system, such as a login.
  • Alert: an event or group of events that a tool flags as worth checking.
  • Incident: a confirmed event that harms, or could harm, the organisation's security.
  • False positive: an alert that looked bad but was harmless. True positive: an alert that was a real problem.
  • IOC (indicator of compromise): a clue that something bad happened, such as a suspicious IP address, domain or file hash.
  • TTP (tactics, techniques and procedures): how an attacker behaves. The MITRE ATT&CK knowledge base catalogues these.

The tools of the trade

  • SIEM (security information and event management): collects logs from many systems into one searchable place and raises alerts. The next guide explains it.
  • EDR (endpoint detection and response): software on laptops and servers that watches for malicious behaviour and can isolate a device.
  • Firewalls and intrusion detection systems: control and watch network traffic.
  • Email security tools: catch phishing and malicious attachments.
  • Ticketing systems: where cases are recorded and handed over.
  • Threat intelligence: information about known bad IP addresses, domains and file hashes.

The product names change from company to company. Learn the ideas first, and the tools become easy to pick up.

How an analyst handles an alert

A simple routine you can follow every time:

ALERT: Multiple failed logins followed by a success
1. What fired?      Rule name, time, affected user and device
2. Is it real?      Does the activity match the user's normal behaviour?
3. How far?         Other accounts, devices or locations involved?
4. What is at risk? Importance of the account and the data it can reach
5. Decide          Close as false positive / escalate / contain
6. Write it down    What you saw, what you checked, what you decided

And a short, clear note of the kind a Tier 2 analyst wants to receive:

Alert: 6 failed logins then 1 success for user "adaeze" from IP 203.0.113.50
Checked: user normally signs in from Lagos; this IP is in another country
Finding: sign-in succeeded after failures, from an unusual location
Decision: ESCALATE to Tier 2 as a possible account compromise
Action taken: asked Tier 2 to confirm with the user and reset the password

The IP address in that note comes from a range reserved for documentation examples, so it belongs to nobody. Always use made-up data when you practise.

Skills to build

  • Networking basics: IP addresses, ports, DNS, HTTP and how traffic flows.
  • Operating systems: how Windows and Linux record user activity.
  • Reading logs and searching them (next guide).
  • Clear writing: your notes are your product.
  • Calm judgement: knowing when to escalate rather than guess.
  • A little scripting in Python or a shell, to save time on repeated checks.

Starting a career

Many people begin in IT support or as a junior analyst. Entry-level certifications that employers often mention include CompTIA Security+, and for analysts, CompTIA CySA+, plus the free introductory certification from ISC2. Certification names and requirements change, so check each provider's own site before you pay for anything. Hands-on practice counts for a lot: keep a notebook of the labs you finish and what you learned.

Work shifts and stress are real. Many SOCs run 24 hours a day, so analysts may work nights and weekends. Learn what a team's schedule looks like before you apply.

Common beginner mistakes

  • Treating every alert as an emergency, or every alert as noise.
  • Deciding without checking. Always look at the evidence first.
  • Poor notes. If you cannot explain it, the next person cannot act on it.
  • Learning tool buttons instead of the ideas behind them.
  • Testing against systems you do not own. Practise only in labs you are allowed to use.

Practice project

Invent an alert, for example a user signing in at 3 a.m. from a new country. Write the six-step triage list for it, decide what you would check, and write a five-line note like the one above. Do the same for two more made-up alerts: a suspicious email attachment and a laptop talking to an unknown server.

Summary

  • A SOC watches an organisation's systems and responds to attacks.
  • Analysts triage alerts, investigate, escalate and document.
  • Know the terms: event, alert, incident, false positive, IOC.
  • Core tools are the SIEM, EDR, network sensors and ticketing.
  • Networking, operating systems, logs and clear writing are your starting skills.
