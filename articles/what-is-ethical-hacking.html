What Is Ethical Hacking? Rules, Methods and Careers

Learn how security testers find weaknesses legally, and the rules that make it ethical.

What is ethical hacking?

Ethical hacking, also called penetration testing or security testing, means looking for weaknesses in a system with the owner's permission, so they can be fixed before a real attacker finds them. The skills are the same ones an attacker uses. What makes it ethical is permission, honesty and the intention to help.

Permission is everything. Testing a system you do not own, without written permission, can be a crime even if you mean well and even if you change nothing. Laws such as the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act in the United States, the Computer Misuse Act in the United Kingdom and the Cybercrimes Act in Nigeria cover this, and other countries have similar laws. This is general information, not legal advice.

Types of hackers

  • White hat: works with permission to improve security.
  • Black hat: breaks in for crime, money or harm.
  • Grey hat: acts without permission but says they mean no harm. Good intentions do not make it legal, so this is the zone to stay out of.

Why organisations hire testers

  • To find weaknesses before criminals do.
  • To check that defences really work.
  • To meet customer or legal requirements.
  • To train the defending team (the "blue team") against a friendly attacking team (the "red team").

The stages of a security test

Although every job is different, most follow this outline:

  1. Planning and scoping: agree what may be tested, when and how, in writing.
  2. Information gathering: learn what the target exposes, such as the pages and services it publishes.
  3. Scanning and analysis: look for known weaknesses, such as outdated software or weak settings.
  4. Testing the weaknesses: carefully check whether a weakness can really be used, without causing harm.
  5. Reporting: describe each finding, its risk and how to fix it.
  6. Retesting: confirm that the fixes worked.

Notice that the first step is paperwork and the last two are about helping the owner. The technical middle is only part of the work.

Scope and rules of engagement

The scope says exactly what you may test. Stay inside it, even when something outside looks tempting. Here is a made-up example of what a scope statement contains: 

Client:        Example Company Ltd (made-up)
Authorised by: Head of IT (name and signature)
Dates:         3 to 7 November, 9:00 to 17:00
In scope:      www.example.test and its login pages
Out of scope:  payment provider, staff email, any other company's systems
Rules:         No denial-of-service. No changing or deleting real data.
               Stop and report at once if sensitive data is exposed.
Contact:       Name and phone number for emergencies

Writing a finding

A finding is only useful if the reader understands it and can act on it. Notice that this example explains the risk and the fix, not just the problem: 

Title:        Login page allows unlimited password attempts
Severity:     Medium
Where:        https://www.example.test/login
What I found: The page accepted 500 wrong passwords in a row with no lock-out.
Why it matters: An attacker could guess weak passwords automatically.
Fix:          Limit attempts, add delays or lock-outs, and require multi-factor sign-in.
Evidence:     Screenshot and request log (attached)

Bug bounty programs

Many companies run bug bounty programs that invite researchers to report weaknesses and sometimes pay rewards. Each program publishes its own rules about what is allowed. Read them completely and follow them exactly. Platforms such as HackerOne and Bugcrowd host many programs. Beginners should expect it to take time and practice before the first accepted report.

Skills to build first

  • Networking: IP, ports, DNS, HTTP.
  • Linux basics and the command line.
  • How websites work: start with the HTML, JavaScript, REST API and database guides on ToolSphere.
  • Scripting: Python or JavaScript, to automate checks.
  • Security fundamentals: passwords, hashing, encryption, authentication.
  • Report writing: clear, honest and calm.

Careers and certifications

Roles include penetration tester, application security engineer, red team member and security consultant. Employers often mention certifications such as CompTIA PenTest+, eJPT, OSCP and CEH, with very different cost and difficulty. Names and requirements change, so check each provider before you commit. Many people start in IT support, development or a SOC and move across.

A professional code of conduct

  • Only test what you are authorised to test.
  • Do the least harm. Never delete or change real data.
  • Keep what you find confidential, and report it only to the owner.
  • Be honest in your reports, including about what you did not test.
  • Stop and tell the owner if you find something dangerous, such as exposed personal data.

Common beginner mistakes

  • Testing "just to see" a website you do not own.
  • Running scanning tools on your school, workplace or public Wi-Fi network.
  • Thinking the tools are the skill. The knowledge behind them is.
  • Writing reports that list problems but do not explain the risk or the fix.
  • Skipping the basics of networking and web technology.

Practice project

Write a scope statement and a finding for an imaginary website, in the style above. Use the .test or example.com style of names so nobody real is involved. Then read the rules of one public bug bounty program and list five things it forbids.

Summary

  • Ethical hacking is authorised, careful security testing to help the owner.
  • Written permission and a clear scope come first, always.
  • Tests follow stages from planning to reporting and retesting.
  • Strong basics in networking, web technology and writing matter more than tools.
